\section*{Discussion}

The lab-built 3D clinostat and the commercial RPM~2.0 produced largely the same transcriptional response in planktonic \textit{M.~marinum}. \nDegShared{} genes changed under both, all in the same direction, and fold changes across the whole transcriptome correlated at $\rho=\rhoSimulators$. A direct comparison found no gene that differed between the machines. This puts numbers on the qualitative conclusion of Clary and colleagues, who reported similar transcriptome changes under the two simulators\cite{clary2022development}, and supports the use of the inexpensive clinostat as a substitute for the RPM under these conditions. The direct comparison is weak, however: with \nRpm{} RPM samples, a modest simulator-specific effect would go undetected. The RPM gave somewhat more DEGs and a stronger respiration signal than the clinostat, which may be a real difference in degree or may simply reflect sampling.

The shared response points to a few functional themes, which rest on mostly electronic annotations and should be read as hypotheses. Among up-regulated genes, Mce-family proteins dominated the extracellular-region signal; in mycobacteria, Mce systems import lipids such as cholesterol\cite{pandey2008mycobacterial}. Complex~I (\textit{nuo}) genes were also up, most clearly under the RPM, and respiration was the only KEGG-mapped subsystem enriched for DEGs. Among down-regulated genes, transcription factors and heat-shock functions stood out: \goGenesHeatSharedDown. One reading is a shift towards respiratory metabolism and lipid uptake together with relaxed protein-quality-control stress in the low-shear, mixed environment of the simulators. These data cannot distinguish an effect of mechanical unloading from indirect effects such as changes in mass transport, oxygen supply or growth rate. Clary and colleagues reported reduced planktonic growth under other clinostat settings\cite{clary2022development}, so differences in growth between conditions cannot be ruled out.

Several functions emphasised in earlier, uncorrected versions of this analysis were not supported by the re-analysis. Mycolic acid and FAS-II genes did not change (\mycolicDegClino{} and \mycolicDegRpm{} DEGs), and neither oxidative-stress nor ESX secretion terms were enriched among the DEGs.

TabPFN, a foundation model built for small tabular data\cite{hollmann2025accurate}, did no better than logistic regression or a random forest at telling microgravity from 1g samples. Its apparent advantage on the three-class task did not hold up in the sensitivity analysis. With eight samples, classification adds little to the differential-expression analysis: the two samples every model misclassified are the same two that sit apart in the PCA.

The data have real limits. The libraries are dominated by rRNA, leaving roughly \cdsPairsPrimaryMinK{}{,}000--\cdsPairsPrimaryMaxK{}{,}000 coding read pairs per sample. The primary analysis has only \nPrimary{} samples, so the co-expression network and its hub genes in particular are exploratory. The RFPRPM4 defect also shows how easily a deposit error propagates. The duplicated file passes every checksum, because the deposited checksum describes the defective file itself, and an analysis that reads it naively silently doubles one sample. Detecting it took comparing files byte by byte. We have prepared a report for the OSDR curators, and we keep the inferred library out of the primary analysis until the depositors confirm it; including it does not change the conclusions (Supplementary Fig.~S1).

In summary, two different microgravity simulators drove the same large transcriptional programme in \textit{M.~marinum}, involving lipid-uptake and respiratory genes up and stress and regulatory genes down.
